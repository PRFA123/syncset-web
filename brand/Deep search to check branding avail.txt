Resumo Compreensivo — Naming da AI Automation Agency

1. Objetivo inicial

O objetivo é encontrar um nome forte, memorável e escalável para uma AI Automation Agency, inicialmente voltada para empresas na Austrália, mas com potencial internacional.

A agência deve poder atender diferentes setores, incluindo:

O nome não deve limitar a empresa a um nicho específico nem apenas a uma tecnologia que pode mudar no futuro.

2. Critérios definidos para o nome

Ao longo da conversa, foram estabelecidos filtros importantes.

O nome ideal deve:

Ser internacional

Funcionar principalmente em inglês, mas sem criar associações negativas ou estranhas em português.

Ser fácil de pronunciar

Especialmente para empresários australianos e clientes B2B.

Ser memorável

Preferencialmente curto e com boa sonoridade.

Parecer uma marca proprietária

O objetivo não é necessariamente usar uma palavra comum do dicionário.

Exemplo de direção desejada:

Tesla, Nvidia, Asana, Palantir

Ou seja, nomes que podem construir significado próprio.

Ser escalável

O nome deve funcionar hoje para:

AI automation;

AI agents;

workflows;

CRM integration.

E no futuro para:

software;

AI infrastructure;

consulting;

SaaS;

autonomous systems;

education.

Evitar excesso de "AI"

A conclusão foi que colocar AI no nome principal pode limitar a marca ao momento tecnológico atual.

Melhor:

BRAND

com AI, automation ou intelligent systems no posicionamento.

2. Metodologia — 8 Gates de Eliminação

Gate Critério Descrição
1 Trademark Busca em IP Australia, USPTO, EUIPO (classes 9, 35, 42). Qualquer marca viva ou pendente = eliminação
2 Phone Test Dito uma vez ao telefone, não pode ter grafia alternativa. Grafias inventadas = eliminação
3 Meaning Deve carregar imagem concreta em inglês na primeira audição. Fragmentos vazios = eliminação
4 No Borrowed Equity Não pode ecoar, truncar ou rimar com empresa existente em tech/automotivo/consultoria/software
5 Solo-Safe Sufixos proibidos: "Partners", "Collective", "Group", "& Co", "Consulting Partners", "Ventures". Permitidos: "Studio", "Lab/Labs", "Works", "Systems", "Automation", "Engine", "Forge", "Desk"
6 Domínio Prioridade: .com → .com.au → .ai → .io. .com é o alvo. .com.au sozinho desqualifica
7 Scarcity Interrogation "Por que está disponível?" Se a resposta honesta for fraqueza, elimine
8 Logo Viability 5-9 letras, 1-3 sílabas, sem diacríticos, hífens ou números, legível a 16px

3. Evolução das Direções de Naming

Fase 1 — Nomes de Resultado (primeira rodada)

· Testados: Nabbed, Nothing Missed, LeadKeeper, OmniSure, Vantage, OnPoint
· Resultado: Nomes de resultado venceram nomes de mecanismo. "Nothing Missed" teve a melhor resposta com trades, mas era de 2 palavras (Gate 8)

Fase 2 — Nomes para Múltiplos Nichos

· Descoberta crítica: O nome precisa servir advogado E encanador. A dor do advogado é depois da recepção, não na chamada perdida.
· Resultado: Nomes focados em telefone foram eliminados. "OmniSure" venceu com a advogada, mas foi rejeitado pelo encanador.

Fase 3 — Nomes Abstratos e de Dicionário

· Testados: Relay, Aegis, Keystone, Axiom, Vertex, Nexus, Apex, Meridian
· Resultado: 100% ocupados. O mercado de .ai e .com está saturado para palavras de dicionário.
· Conclusão: Criar nomes compostos ou usar neologismos.

Fase 4 — Objetos Concretos (a virada)

· Lógica: Fundadores de tech foram atrás de conceitos gregos/latinos. Ninguém pegou objetos físicos.
· Testados: Keel, Rivet, Brace, Shim, Spindle, Clamp, Pivot
· Resultado: A maioria ocupada, mas a lógica era correta.

Fase 5 — Nomes Concretos + Sufixos Permitidos (estratégia atual)

· Lógica: Palavra concreta + "Works", "Forge", "Labs", "Studio"
· Exemplo: Tiller Works (leme + obras)
· Vantagem: Domínio .ai com alta chance de disponibilidade, sem conflito de marca.

Fase 6 — Nomes em Português/Espanhol e Iniciais Pessoais

· Testados: Âncora, Cais, Farol, Marco, Rede, Pafand (iniciais)
· Resultado: Eliminados no Gate 2 (Phone Test) e Gate 3 (Meaning)

Trademark and domain audit
Important limitation
A meaningful clearance opinion cannot be delivered from name creativity alone. It requires searching the relevant databases for exact marks, phonetic equivalents, misspellings, similar visual marks, related services, business names, marketplace usage, and relevant jurisdictions.

In Australia, trade-mark classes organise claims: Classes 1–34 cover goods and Classes 35–45 cover services. For an AI automation firm, Class 9 commonly covers downloadable software, while Class 42 commonly covers software development and non-downloadable software/SaaS. IP Australia provides both a TM Checker tool and the Australian Trade Mark Search for registered and pending marks. The same Nice classification structure is used internationally; Class 35 covers advertising/business services and Class 42 covers computer and scientific services.

Core filing classes
Class	Why it matters	Illustrative claim territory
9	Protects downloadable software, applications, AI software, recorded programs, and data-processing software	Downloadable AI software; workflow-automation software; computer software for business-process management
35	Covers business consulting, business management, business process optimisation, and operational consulting	Business consultancy relating to digital transformation and process efficiency
42	Core class for SaaS, software development, technology consulting, hosting, AI design, integration, and managed technical services	SaaS featuring AI workflow automation; design and development of AI software; IT consultancy; cloud computing
38	Consider if you operate communications, messaging, or collaboration infrastructure as a service	Providing access to platforms for digital communication
41	Consider for paid training, education, certification, workshops, or an academy	Training in AI, automation, and digital transformation

For a typical agency that may become a managed AI platform company, a focused initial portfolio is usually Classes 9, 35, and 42, with 38 and 41 added only if they are commercially material. Filing unnecessary classes adds cost and creates vulnerability if there is no bona fide use plan.

Audit protocol per name
For each shortlisted candidate, document the following before design, incorporation, or public launch:

Exact-word search
Search the full wordmark in each registry and Google/Bing. Record active, pending, expired, opposed, cancelled, and abandoned results, while focusing particularly on active/pending marks.

Similarity search
Search likely phonetic, plural, spacing, suffix, and spelling variants. For example, for “Orchestriq,” test orchestriq, orchestric, orchestriq, orchestra, orchestration, orchestrate, and compound forms.

Class relevance review
A similar mark in an unrelated category might be acceptable; a weaker similarity in Classes 9, 35, or 42 may be more problematic. Assess relatedness of services, customers, channels, geography, and likelihood of buyer confusion.

Common-law review
Search the web, LinkedIn, Crunchbase, app marketplaces, GitHub, software directories, Australian business registries, and social networks. Registered trade-mark databases are necessary but not the complete risk picture.

Domain review
Check the exact .com, .com.au, .ai, .io, and relevant defensive variants through an accredited registrar and ICANN Lookup. ICANN’s lookup service provides registration data for domain names and internet-number resources.

Linguistic and cultural review
Test pronunciation and unintended meanings with Australian, North American, UK/EU, and priority APAC audiences. Verify that it is easy to say on a sales call and easy to type after hearing it.

Attorney opinion
Have Australian counsel issue a clearance assessment and filing strategy. If US/EU expansion is planned, obtain jurisdiction-specific advice before committing to public use.

Trademark-risk framework
Use this model to classify results rather than relying on a simplistic “available/unavailable” verdict:

Risk	Definition	Action
Low	No materially similar active/pending marks in adjacent classes or markets; limited conflicting use	Proceed to legal clearance and filing
Moderate	Similar elements, but different overall impression, goods/services, or geography	Obtain attorney advice; consider logo and specification strategy
High	Exact or strongly similar active/pending marks in Classes 9, 35, 42, or adjacent software/consulting services	Remove from shortlist unless counsel identifies a clear path
Critical	Exact matching mark or strong common-law competitor in the same market and services	Do not adopt
.com availability framework
Do not represent a domain as available until it is checked live at the time of purchase. Domain status can change within minutes, privacy services may obscure registrants, and an available domain does not solve trademark conflict.

Domain outcome	Assessment	Recommended response
Exact .com available	Best case	Secure immediately after legal go-ahead; also secure .com.au, .ai, and common misspellings
Exact .com parked or unused	Potentially workable	Investigate price and registrant; set a walk-away price; do not assume acquisition is possible
Exact .com held by unrelated active business	Moderate to high brand risk	Prefer a different name unless the business is clearly remote and counsel approves
Exact .com held by adjacent technology or consulting business	High risk	Reject candidate
Only modified domain available	Acceptable only if the master brand is strong and exact .com is not used by a confusingly similar business	Prefer clean modifiers such as get[name].com, [name]systems.com, or [name]group.com; avoid hyphens and awkward spellings

Trademark audit framework (Classes 9, 35, 42)
For an AI automation agency that may become a software-enabled services company, the core Nice classes are:

Class 9 – Downloadable software, apps, AI software, data-processing programs.

Class 35 – Business consulting, management, process optimisation, operational consulting.

Class 42 – SaaS, software development, AI design, integration, hosting, managed technical services.

IP Australia and other offices use the Nice Classification to organise goods and services into 45 classes, with Classes 1–34 for goods and 35–45 for services. For software delivered as a service, Class 42 is canonical; for downloadable apps and software products, Class 9 is canonical; many AI/SaaS businesses need both.

Step 1 – Knock-out search in key registries
For each shortlisted name, run:

Australia – IP Australia Trade Mark Search

Use the official search to find existing registered and pending marks.

Search: exact word, common misspellings, plural/singular, and phonetic variants.

Filter to Classes 9, 35, 42 (and adjacent classes if relevant).

WIPO Global Brand Database

Search international registrations and participating national/regional collections.

Use keyword, phonetic, and “similar name” functions where available.

United States – USPTO Trademark Search

Search the federal database for live applications and registrations.

Focus on marks that are live, in related software/consulting/SaaS services, and confusingly similar in sound/meaning.

Optional but recommended

EUIPO eSearch plus / TMview for EU and participating offices.

UKIPO, NZ, Singapore if those markets matter.

IP Australia explicitly advises searching before applying, and its TM Checker gives an early indication (Australia-only) of potential conflicts.

Step 2 – Similarity and class-scope review
For each potentially conflicting mark, ask:

Is the mark live (active application or registration)?

Are the goods/services related to yours (AI, workflow automation, SaaS, consulting, integration)?

Would an enterprise buyer likely think the services come from the same source or are affiliated?

Is the similarity in sound, appearance, or concept strong enough to create confusion?

USPTO guidance emphasises that confusion can arise even across different classes if the services are related and the marks look/sound alike or create a similar commercial impression.

Step 3 – Common-law and marketplace search
Even if a name is not registered, existing market use can block or complicate your path:

Google/Bing the exact name plus terms like “AI”, “automation”, “software”, “consulting”, “SaaS”.

Check LinkedIn, Crunchbase, GitHub, major software directories, app stores, and Australian business registries.

Look for companies offering overlapping services under the same or very similar names.

Step 4 – Attorney clearance opinion
Once you have search results:

Engage an Australian trade-mark attorney to:

Interpret similarity and class overlap.

Advise on filing strategy (word mark vs. logo, specification wording, classes).

Assess risk for your target expansion markets (US, EU, APAC).

IP Australia’s classification guidance notes you should cover your core goods and services without over-filing internal functions; for an AI agency, that usually means 9, 35, and 42 as the foundation.

Domain and URL availability framework
A domain being free does not mean the name is legally clear, but a taken .com can materially affect brand viability, especially for enterprise buyers.

Extensions to check
For each name, check at minimum:

.com – Global primary.

.com.au – Australian trust and local search (requires Australian presence or trade mark).

.ai – Strong signal for AI, useful defensively and for product domains.

.io – Common for technical/software brands.

Use a domain checker or registrar search (GoDaddy, Namecheap, Cloudflare, etc.) to see live availability and pricing.

How to interpret domain results
Domain outcome	Interpretation	Recommended action
Exact .com available	Best-case scenario	Secure immediately after legal go-ahead; also secure .com.au, .ai, .io
Exact .com parked/unused	Potentially workable	Investigate owner and price via WHOIS; set a walk-away budget
Exact .com used by unrelated business	Moderate risk	Assess industry overlap; if far removed and counsel is comfortable, proceed with caution
Exact .com used by adjacent tech/consulting/AI business	High risk	Prefer a different name
Only modified domains available	Acceptable if brand is strong	Use clean modifiers: get[name].com, [name]systems.com, [name]group.com
ICANN’s lookup tool can provide current registration data for domain names, which helps you understand ownership and status when a domain is taken.
