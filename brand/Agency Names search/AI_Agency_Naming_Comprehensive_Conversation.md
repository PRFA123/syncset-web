## 1. Core objective for the agency

The agency is intended to become a scalable AI automation company/startup. The underlying business ambition is not merely to sell AI tools, but to:

- prosper financially;
- help other business owners succeed in their own business journey;
- build practical systems that improve business operations and growth;
- create a company capable of scaling beyond being a traditional automation agency;
- potentially evolve into SaaS, a technology platform, an AI/agent infrastructure business, or a broader business systems company.

A key strategic conclusion from the conversation was that **AI is the mechanism, not necessarily the brand promise**. The brand can therefore focus on outcomes such as growth, efficiency, progress, momentum, prosperity, coordination, and business improvement.

The user's stated dream for the agency was:

> “meu sonho com essa agencia eh prosperar financeiramente e ajudar outros business owners a terem sucesso na jornada.”

This became an important naming criterion: the name should be compatible with both the founder's financial ambition and a genuine customer-success mission.

## 13. Professional naming engineer framework requested by the user

The user asked what a professional branding / business naming engineer would actually research before choosing a startup name intended to scale rapidly.

The following framework was established.

### 1. Positioning before naming

Define:

- what the company is today;
- what it can become later;
- what it should never look like;
- who the core customer is;
- what business outcome is promised.

The name must follow positioning rather than compensate for unclear positioning.

### 2. Market and competitor research

Study:

- direct AI automation agencies;
- automation consultants;
- AI system integrators;
- SaaS and AI platforms;
- successful technology brands in adjacent categories;
- naming density and saturation.

The objective is not to imitate competitors but to understand the naming landscape and avoid entering an already crowded semantic territory.

### 3. Define naming territories

Rather than generating random words, establish 5-7 strategic territories such as:

- systems;
- motion;
- engineering;
- science;
- intelligence;
- growth;
- prosperity;
- abstract / coined brand language.

### 4. Linguistic and phonetic research

Evaluate:

- ease of pronunciation;
- spelling predictability;
- syllable count;
- sound quality;
- telephone / word-of-mouth clarity;
- whether customers can type it correctly after hearing it once.

The preferred general range identified was approximately 2-3 syllables.

### 5. Semantic research

Check:

- meaning in English;
- meanings in Portuguese;
- meanings in likely future markets;
- slang;
- negative associations;
- accidental references to unrelated industries.

The “Baton” rejection demonstrated why this is essential.

### 6. Sonic profile

Assess whether the name sounds:

- strong;
- premium;
- technological;
- trustworthy;
- modern;
- credible;
- or instead cheap, generic, overly industrial, childish, or gimmicky.

### 7. Distinctiveness and trademark potential

Classify the name conceptually as:

- generic;
- descriptive;
- suggestive;
- arbitrary;
- coined / invented.

Distinctive coined or suggestive names generally provide a stronger basis for building a unique brand, subject to actual legal searching.

### 8. Competitor / conflict screening

Search:

- exact names;
- plural / singular variants;
- phonetic variants;
- alternate spellings;
- related domains;
- closely related industries;
- software repositories and AI products.

### 9. Australian trademark research

A serious process should use IP Australia resources and not rely only on Google searches.

The conversation referenced:

- Australian Trade Mark Search: `https://search.ipaustralia.gov.au/trademarks`
- IP Australia TM Checker: `https://www.ipaustralia.gov.au/tm-checker`

A key lesson from the research was that similar registered or pending marks can create registration or infringement problems. Preliminary checks are useful, but they are not a substitute for professional legal advice where the company is serious about long-term trademark protection.

### 10. Relevant trademark classes

For an AI automation company, trademark planning should consider categories relevant to:

- software;
- SaaS / technology services;
- AI systems;
- business services;
- consulting / automation services;
- potentially training or other future offerings.

Exact class selection should be based on the company's planned products and services and verified with current Australian trademark guidance or professional advice.

### 11. Domain research

Preferred hierarchy discussed:

1. `.com`
2. `.ai` when strategically appropriate
3. `.com.au` for the Australian operating entity
4. other extensions only when they support the brand strategy.

A domain can be useful, but the domain should not dictate a weak company name.

### 12. Social handle research

Check whether the brand can maintain reasonably consistent handles across major social and business platforms.

### 13. Searchability / SEO noise

A good name should not be dominated by an unrelated famous term, massive company, generic word, or established search category.

The objective is a realistic path toward owning search results for the company's brand.

### 14. Confusion test

Ask whether a prospective customer could reasonably confuse the name with:

- another AI company;
- a software product;
- a consulting company;
- an unrelated but famous brand.

### 15. Elevator pitch test

Test phrases such as:

> “We’re [NAME].”

and

> “[NAME] helps businesses automate and grow.”

The name should sound natural when spoken by a founder, sales representative, employee, or customer.

### 16. Scalability test

Ask whether the name still works if the company becomes:

- a $100M+ organization;
- a SaaS company;
- an AI agent platform;
- an automation infrastructure company;
- a broader business technology company.

A name that locks the business into a small service category is strategically weaker.

### 17. Brand architecture test

Check whether the name can support future offerings such as:

- [Brand] AI
- [Brand] Systems
- [Brand] Agents
- [Brand] Labs
- [Brand] OS
- [Brand] Automation
- [Brand] Platform

### 18. Visual identity test

Imagine the name as:

- a logo;
- favicon;
- website header;
- LinkedIn company page;
- invoice;
- software interface;
- pitch deck;
- mobile app icon.

A good verbal name should also have strong visual potential.

### 19. Memory test

Ask people to recall the name after roughly 24 hours.

The test is especially useful for detecting names that initially sound clever but are not memorable.

### 20. Target customer test

The name should make sense to the intended market, including the kinds of business owners being considered for the agency.

Examples discussed as target contexts include:

- tradies / trades businesses;
- hospitality;
- professional services;
- wellness businesses;
- other small and medium business owners.

The name should feel credible to a business owner while still being sophisticated enough for future enterprise or technology positioning.

## 15. Recommended professional naming process

The conversation converged on this practical sequence:

1. Brand strategy
2. Naming brief
3. Competitor landscape
4. Define 5-7 naming territories
5. Generate approximately 100-200 candidates
6. Filter to approximately 25-30
7. Apply the weighted scorecard
8. Conduct linguistic / phonetic / cultural screening
9. Conduct competitor and conflict screening
10. Evaluate searchability
11. Check domains
12. Check social handles
13. Conduct trademark research
14. Reduce to 3-5 finalists
15. Run a real-world customer / memory test
16. Select the final name and build the visual identity

This process was presented as the professional alternative to simply brainstorming names until one “sounds cool.”

## 18. What was learned from the conflicts

A recurring pattern in the research was that attractive invented names can already exist in adjacent technology markets.

Therefore the process must test beyond obvious exact matches.

A serious screening should include:

- exact match;
- pluralization;
- suffix and prefix variants;
- phonetic similarity;
- domain variants;
- software companies;
- SaaS products;
- GitHub repositories;
- AI companies;
- automation consultancies;
- Australian businesses;
- international companies in likely expansion markets.

This is particularly important because the agency is intended to scale rapidly. A naming conflict discovered after substantial investment in website, content, software, customer acquisition, and brand recognition can become extremely expensive to fix.

## 20. Broader startup naming principles derived from the research

The research suggested several useful trends for modern technology startups:

### Brand-first naming

Strong technology brands do not always need to explain their product literally in the company name.

### Avoid generic AI naming

Examples of strategically weak patterns include constructions that are essentially:

- AI + generic noun;
- SmartAI Solutions;
- AI Automation Experts;
- GPT + generic descriptor;
- LLM + generic descriptor.

Such patterns can communicate the category but often reduce distinctiveness and long-term brand flexibility.

### Prefer distinctive but pronounceable names

A coined name can be powerful when it is:

- easy to say;
- easy enough to spell;
- memorable;
- visually strong;
- legally screenable;
- broad enough for future products.

### Do not over-optimize for domain hacks

It is better to have a strong company name with an acceptable domain strategy than a weak company name selected only because a short domain was available.

## 21. Strategic conclusion

The naming challenge is not simply to find a word that sounds technologically sophisticated.

The strongest concept emerging from the conversation is to build a brand around the idea that **intelligent systems create movement and leverage, and that leverage helps businesses prosper**.

That leads to a core semantic equation:

**AI / Automation -> Intelligent Systems -> Less Friction -> More Leverage -> Business Growth -> Prosperity**

This gives the agency freedom to begin as an AI automation service while preserving the option to evolve into a larger technology company.

The most promising naming territories therefore remain:

1. **Growth / prosperity** - the outcome for business owners.
2. **Movement / momentum / forward progress** - the operational transformation.
3. **Systems / engineering / science** - the mechanism and credibility.
4. **Abstract coined brand** - the highest flexibility for future scale.

The ideal final name should sit at the intersection of these territories without being overly literal.
